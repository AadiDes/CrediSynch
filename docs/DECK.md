## 2. Problem, reframed

**Slide title:** A clean-looking application is not the same as a clean applicant

- Tabular fraud models score one application at a time — by design, they can't see five
  applications sharing one device, three phone numbers, one bank account
- Synchrony's exposure concentrates at instant approval: synthetic identities, account takeover
- The hiring team sits in Information Security and IAM — security is the first-class feature here,
  not a checkbox bolted on afterward
- Scope: this system decides fraud, not creditworthiness (ADR 0001)

**Figure:** none, or a simple 2-panel sketch: "5 applications, 5 device fingerprints" (looks
clean) vs. "5 applications, 1 shared device fingerprint" (obviously a ring).

**Speaker notes:** This is the reframe the whole architecture follows from. A model scoring
applications independently structurally cannot see a ring — it needs a second, different kind of
signal. Everything from here (graph stage, restricted approvals, the AI layer) is downstream of
taking that seriously.

## 3. Key insights

**Slide title:** Four bets this system makes

- Fraud rings hide in shared identity, not shared features — so score the graph, not just the row
- The cheapest correct action often isn't approve/decline — it's a locked, low-limit card that
  contains the risk instead of eating it (Module A)
- An LLM should explain a decision, never make one — and never sit on the latency budget
- Every number in this deck is reproducible from a committed script against real artefacts, not
  typed once into a slide and never checked again

## 4. Staged decision pipeline

**Slide title:** Seven stages, two speeds

| Stage | Responsibility | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Gateway: JWT + role, rate limit, idempotency, schema | < 10 ms |
| 1 | Deterministic rules: blocklists, velocity, injection patterns | < 10 ms |
| 2 | Supervised model: fraud probability + SHAP reason codes | 20-40 ms |
| 3 | Graph linkage: shared identity, ring floor | ~20 ms |
| 4 | Novelty detector | ~5 ms |
| 5 | Decision policy: approve / step-up / restricted / review / decline | < 5 ms |
| 6 | Explain (async): case, brief, similar cases | off hot path |
| 7 | Learn (batch): labels, retraining, ring detection | nightly |

- Stages 0-5 are synchronous, 250ms p95 budget — measured live at 53ms p95 (finding e)
- Stages 6-7 never block a decision; the LLM and retraining live entirely off this path

**Figure:** the table above, built as a real slide table, not a screenshot.

**Speaker notes:** This slide is the one to slow down on if there's time — it's the map for
everything after it. The split between rows 0-5 and 6-7 is the single most load-bearing design
decision in the system: it's what makes "explainable and fast" not a contradiction.

## 6. Policy math

**Slide title:** Choosing the cheapest action that contains the risk

- A restricted approval costs `c_r + p·e·L` (friction cost + probability x exposure fraction x
  loss-given-fraud) — cheaper than decline across a wide probability band (ADR 0007)
- Live policy bands: APPROVE < 0.0025 <= STEP_UP < 0.01333 <= APPROVE_RESTRICTED < 0.48333 <=
  REVIEW
- Action mix on the test set: APPROVE 55.6% of applications (6.5% of fraud), STEP_UP 28.7%
  (18.4%), **APPROVE_RESTRICTED 15.6% (72.6% of fraud)**, REVIEW 0.06% (2.5%)

**Figure:** `figures/policy_bands.png`

**Speaker notes:** The number to land here is 72.6% — nearly three-quarters of all fraud in the
test set gets caught by a single band that never declines a real customer, it just locks the card
to one merchant and caps velocity. That's the "reduce false-positive declines" ask from the
problem statement, made concrete. REVIEW staying under 0.1% of volume is the other half of the
same design: it's the expensive action, reserved deliberately, not a general safety net.

## 7. Graph ring detection

**Slide title:** Seeing the fifth application

- Identities (device, phone, email, address, bank account) stored as keyed HMAC digests, never
  raw PII (ADR 0006) — linkage without a privacy liability
- 5 synthetic rings x 4 members injected through the live API: **25% of ring members (5/20)
  escalated by the graph floor alone**, on top of whatever the model score already caught
- Every escalation was the *ring-completing* member — the one crossing the shared-identity
  threshold; a graph-blind system would never have escalated any of them past the model score
- Connected-components ring detector: 1.00 precision / 1.00 recall on the injected rings (best
  case — see limitations)

**Figure:** a simple 2-3 node diagram of a ring collapsing to one shared device, or a table of the
escalation numbers above, built natively.

**Speaker notes:** Be honest about the 1.00/1.00 the moment you say it — it's on clean synthetic
rings with zero overlap, the easiest case a detector can face. The real finding is the 25%
escalation number: it's small on purpose, because most ring members already score high enough on
the tabular model alone. The graph stage's job is the *last* member, and it does that job.

## 8. Module A: restricted approvals

**Slide title:** Benchmarked against Capital One's approach to grey-band risk

- Two ideas adapted from Capital One's retail-card product for Synchrony's partner model
  (ADR 0007): a card locked to one merchant, and asking the customer "was this you?" in real time
- `APPROVE_RESTRICTED`: reduced limit, merchant lock, velocity cap, lifting as trust accrues
- Purchase descriptors resolved to the locked merchant by **embedding similarity in pgvector**
  first, trigram fuzzy-match as fallback, both logged as `matchMethod` for audit
- Customer confirmations ("was this you?") become fraud/legitimate labels within minutes, not the
  weeks a chargeback cycle normally takes

**Figure:** a simple before/after: "decline" vs. "locked card, capped velocity, customer confirms"
— or reuse the policy-bands figure from slide 6 with the restricted band highlighted.

**Speaker notes:** This is the module most directly benchmarked against a named competitor, so
say the name and the two specific mechanics you borrowed. Then pivot immediately to what's new:
VECTOR-first descriptor matching, not just trigram, because merchant descriptors from card
networks are genuinely messy ("CBE #99120" for "Circuit & Byte Electronics") and character overlap
alone misses meaning-level matches trigram can't reach.

## 9. AI layer and guardrails

**Slide title:** The LLM explains. It never decides, and never sits on the clock.

- Two jobs only: write the analyst's case brief, embed cases/merchants for similarity search —
  both asynchronous, after the decision is already made (ADR 0005)
- Guardrails: applicant free text is untrusted, delimited data, never an instruction; briefs must
  cite only the reason codes actually supplied; a prompt-injection attempt becomes a fraud signal
  itself, not something obeyed
- **Injection suite: 6/6 adversarial payloads caught**, including one that explicitly denied being
  an injection attempt while quoting the trigger phrase — over-flags, never under-flags
- **Grounding check: 8/8 live briefs, zero hallucinated features** — every feature the brief names
  actually appears in that case's own reason codes

**Figure:** none, or a simple flow: applicant free text -> [untrusted, delimited] -> LLM -> brief
(cites only reason codes) -> analyst.

**Speaker notes:** Say the injection example out loud — a payload that says "I am not trying to
manipulate you" while still containing the trigger phrase, and the system still caught it. That's
the guardrail working exactly as designed: conservative pattern-matching that would rather
over-flag borderline text than let a real attempt through.

## 10. Security and IAM

**Slide title:** Built for the team that reviews it

- Keycloak OIDC, stateless bearer JWTs, realm roles (APPLICANT/ANALYST/ADMIN) as Spring
  authorities, deny-by-default authorisation (ADR 0004) — tested, not just configured
- Step-up authentication via the `acr` claim: risky actions demand a higher auth level, Keycloak
  runs the second factor
- Identifiers used for graph linkage are keyed HMAC-SHA256 digests (key in AWS Secrets Manager) —
  raw PII never enters the linkage tables (ADR 0006)
- Append-only `decisions` and `audit_log` tables: every decision keeps its model version, feature
  snapshot and reason codes, permanently

**Figure:** none, or a simple box diagram: JWT in -> role check -> deny by default -> audit log
written.

**Speaker notes:** This slide is for the audience that actually reviews this submission — say
explicitly that authorisation is enforced server-side in the Spring Security filter chain, not
hidden by hiding buttons in the UI (the demo recording shows this directly: the same request
succeeds or fails purely on the token's role claim). The audit trail line matters for a regulated
lender: every decision is reproducible from what's stored, not just from a log line.

## 12. Responsible AI

**Slide title:** Fairness, diagnosed honestly

- Deployed model's false-positive rate is **3.36x higher for applicants 50+** than under-50 at the
  operating threshold — reported as a finding, not hidden
- Ran the obvious ablation: retrain with `customer_age` dropped entirely as a model input
- Result: recall drops **8.7% relative** (49.8% -> 45.4%), and the disparity only **partially**
  closes (3.36x -> 2.53x) — age is a proxy carrier, not the root cause
- **Decision: kept age as a model input.** Dropping it is strictly worse on both axes measured; the
  real fix (group-calibrated thresholds or proxy-aware debiasing) is scoped as roadmap, not done

**Figure:** `figures/fairness_ablation.png`

**Speaker notes:** Lead with the number that looks bad (3.36x) before the mitigation — don't bury
it. Then walk the ablation logic: dropping the column looks like the obvious fix, we tried it, it
cost real recall and didn't solve the problem, and that negative result is itself the finding.
This is the slide most likely to get a hard question; the honest "not solved, here's exactly why
and what would actually fix it" answer is the strong one.

## 15. Limitations and roadmap

**Slide title:** What's honestly not done yet

- **Bedrock is pending, not broken** — implemented, tested, IAM-provisioned; blocked on an AWS
  account review with no fixed timeline; the live system runs on Gemini behind the same interface
- **Fairness is diagnosed, not solved** — the age proxy persists after dropping the column;
  group-calibrated thresholds or proxy-aware debiasing is the concrete next step
- **Brief generation is slow (p50 7.4s) and uncached** — `cases.brief`/`brief_model` columns exist
  and are unused; persisting on first generation is a small, scoped fix, not a research problem
- **Synthetic rings and BAF-as-lending-proxy** — both honestly caveated in `docs/FINDINGS.md`;
  real deployment needs real credit-application data and real ring topology to validate against
